12 Sleep Hygiene Tips for Restless Nights (That Actually Fit Real Life)

S

By Savv
September 2026 · 7 min read

Most sleep advice assumes you live in a silent cabin with blackout shutters and no responsibilities. This is sleep hygiene for the rest of us: small, doable tweaks for people whose brains won’t shut up at midnight.

A note from Sock: Sock is a duck, not a doctor. These are general sleep-habit ideas for informational purposes only, not medical advice. If you snore loudly, gasp in your sleep, or struggle with persistent insomnia, talk to a qualified health professional; those can be signs of conditions worth checking out.

The core idea: teach your body that night is coming

Your body runs on cues. Light, temperature, activity, and routine all whisper “it’s daytime” or “it’s nighttime” to your brain. Restless sleep usually isn’t one big problem. It’s a pile of daytime cues accidentally shouting at bedtime. You don’t need to fix all of them. Pick two or three and start there.

Wind-down tweaks (the evening shift)

  1. Dim the lights an hour before bed. Bright overhead light tells your brain it’s noon. Lamps instead of ceiling lights is enough. No special bulbs required.
  2. Park your phone outside the bedroom. Or at least across the room, face-down, on Do Not Disturb. The scroll hole is the number-one sleep thief, and willpower at 11 p.m. is a myth.
  3. Keep the room cool. Your core temperature needs to drop to fall asleep. Slightly cool (around 18–20°C / 65–68°F) works for most people. Crack a window or ditch a blanket layer.
  4. Write tomorrow’s worries down. Keep a notepad by the bed. When your brain starts rehearsing Tuesday’s meeting, dump it on paper. Your brain lets go of things it trusts are captured.
  5. Build a 20-minute landing strip. Same low-key sequence nightly: change clothes, wash up, read a few pages, lights out. Boring is the point. Routine is a lullaby for adults.

Daytime habits that secretly run your nights

  1. Get morning light. Ten minutes of daylight before noon sets your internal clock more powerfully than anything you do at night. Step outside with your coffee.
  2. Cut caffeine by early afternoon. Caffeine’s half-life is around 5–6 hours, so that 4 p.m. coffee is still half-active at 10 p.m. Try a noon cutoff for a week and see.
  3. Move your body, just not right before bed. Regular daytime movement is linked to better sleep quality. Vigorous exercise within 2 hours of bedtime can be stimulating for some people, so earlier is safer.
  4. Watch the nightcap. Alcohol can make you drowsy, but it fragments sleep later in the night. If you drink, earlier in the evening and modest amounts tend to disrupt less.
  5. Keep naps short and early. A 20-minute nap before 3 p.m. is refreshing; a 90-minute couch session at 6 p.m. is a loan shark charging interest at midnight.

For the 3 a.m. wide-awake club

  1. Don’t lie there stewing. If you’re awake and frustrated for more than ~20 minutes, get up, go somewhere dim, and do something dull (read a boring book, fold laundry). Return when sleepy. Training your brain that bed = sleep, not bed = worrying, matters.
  2. Stop clock-watching. Turn the alarm clock around. Knowing it’s 3:47 a.m. adds performance anxiety to wakefulness, which is the opposite of helpful.

The duck’s realistic take

You will not do all twelve. Nobody does. The people who sleep better are the ones who picked two (usually the phone boundary and the caffeine cutoff) and did them consistently for a couple of weeks. Small levers, big doors.

And if you’ve tried the basics for a few weeks and sleep is still rough (loud snoring, gasping, or insomnia that won’t budge), that’s a conversation for a real doctor or sleep clinic, not a duck blog. No shame in it; that’s what they’re for.
